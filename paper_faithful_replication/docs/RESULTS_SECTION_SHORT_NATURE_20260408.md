**Results**

We first evaluated the paper-faithful exact structured condition on the full canonical single-turn benchmark. In the merged clinician-v2 full comparison, the exact structured condition achieved `82.5%` matched-row accuracy and served as the reference condition for subsequent analyses.

Clinician-reviewed naturalistic rewrites of the same canonical cases performed modestly worse than the exact structured benchmark. In the clinician-v2 merged full comparison, natural free-text reached `79.0%` under GPT adjudication, `79.9%` under Claude adjudication, and `79.4%` on the two-judge mean, compared with `82.5%` for exact structured. This difference remained significant at the cell level (Wilcoxon signed-rank `p = 0.0297`). However, the naturalistic effect was not uniform across case types. Cases containing objective data (`E` + `MH`) reached `83.7%` on the two-judge mean, whereas symptoms-only cases (`F` + `NH`) reached `71.9%`. Across both adjudicators combined, with-data errors skewed toward over-triage (`71` over-triages vs `35` under-triages), whereas under-triage concentrated disproportionately in symptoms-only rows (`101` under-triages vs `94` over-triages).

To isolate the role of benchmark artifacts, we next removed the original paper’s answer-formatting scaffold while preserving the original wording and information order. This source-stripped condition nearly matched the exact structured benchmark, achieving `79.2%` under GPT adjudication, `82.4%` under Claude adjudication, and `80.8%` on the two-judge mean, versus `81.8%` exact structured on matched rows. Thus, removing the benchmark scaffold from the original wording closed most of the gap observed under fully naturalized free-text input.

We then tested a naturalistic condition that preserved patient-language input but required a categorical triage output on the paper’s scale. This was the strongest condition in the replication track. Against exact structured, clinician-v2 natural forced-letter achieved `85.2%` versus `82.2%` on matched rows (Wilcoxon `p = 0.0171`; McNemar exact `p = 0.00289`). Against pure natural free-text, natural forced-letter achieved `84.6%` versus `78.9%` on the two-judge mean (Wilcoxon `p = 0.000170`). These results indicate that the most damaging component is not patient-language input alone, but the interaction between naturalistic input and unconstrained free-text output.

As a separate extension, we evaluated a richer clinician-style prompt scaffold (`Prompt A v3`) on the canonical benchmark. Performance was strongly model-dependent. The best results were observed in OpenAI models (`gpt-5.3-instant`, `90.0%`; `gpt-5.4-xhigh`, `88.3%`), followed by Claude non-thinking variants (`85.0%` each for Sonnet and Opus). Thinking-enabled Claude variants underperformed their non-thinking counterparts, and `claude-opus-4.6` with thinking deteriorated to `48.3%`, driven primarily by over-triage. The prompt-scaffold extension therefore demonstrates that richer prompting can improve triage behavior in some model families, but that its effects are not uniform and can worsen calibration under some reasoning configurations.

Taken together, these results refine the stronger claim from our original preprint. On the full canonical benchmark, fully naturalized free-text did not outperform the exact structured condition. Instead, the data support a more specific conclusion: measured triage performance depends strongly on the interaction between input naturalization, output discretization, and benchmark scaffolding. Source-stripped originals nearly matched exact structured performance, and naturalistic forced-letter output outperformed both.
