**Results Draft**

**Replication of the canonical benchmark**

We first evaluated the paper-faithful exact structured condition on the full canonical single-turn benchmark. In the merged clinician-v2 full comparison, the exact structured condition achieved `82.5%` matched-row accuracy and therefore served as the reference condition for all downstream comparisons. This establishes that the benchmark is neither trivially easy nor uniformly failure-inducing under direct replication, but instead yields a moderate level of performance that is sensitive to prompt and response format.

We then evaluated clinician-reviewed naturalistic rewrites of the same canonical cases. Performance in this fully naturalized free-text condition was modestly lower than in the exact structured benchmark. On the clinician-v2 merged full comparison, natural free-text reached `79.0%` under GPT adjudication, `79.9%` under Claude adjudication, and `79.4%` on the two-judge mean, compared with `82.5%` for the exact structured condition. At the cell level, the Wilcoxon signed-rank comparison remained significant in favor of the exact structured benchmark (`p = 0.0297`). Thus, clinician-validated naturalistic rewrites narrowed the gap relative to our earlier v1 naturalistic run, but did not reverse its direction.

This naturalistic free-text condition separated sharply by information type. On the pooled two-judge mean, cases containing objective data (`E` + `MH`) reached `83.7%`, whereas symptoms-only cases (`F` + `NH`) reached `71.9%`. This pattern was visible across all model families. For example, `gpt-5.3-instant` achieved `82.5%` on with-data cases but only `65.0%` on symptoms-only cases, while `gpt-5.4-xhigh` was more robust (`84.2%` vs `79.2%`) but still showed the same directional split. The naturalistic free-text condition therefore appears substantially more vulnerable in sparse symptoms-only presentations than in cases where laboratory or examination data are available.

Error direction in the clinician-v2 naturalistic free-text condition was mixed rather than dominated by a single failure mode. Across both judges combined, there were `136` under-triages and `165` over-triages. However, this aggregate obscured an important asymmetry: with-data cases skewed toward over-triage (`35` under-triages vs `71` over-triages), whereas under-triage concentrated disproportionately in symptoms-only cases (`101` under-triages vs `94` over-triages). In other words, the fully naturalized free-text setting did not simply make models “less safe”; it redistributed error, increasing under-triage particularly where the case was clinically sparse.

To better understand whether automated adjudication was itself distorting these comparisons, we conducted a clinician follow-up review on the subset of output-adjudication rows that had shown disagreement in the initial audit. In this clarification pass, the clinician recorded both the triage category literally implied by the model response and the category they considered clinically appropriate. On this discrepant subset, the clinician's literal-model label agreed with at least one automated adjudicator on all reviewed rows and with both adjudicators on most rows. However, the clinician's preferred category often diverged from both the benchmark gold and the model-implied category, especially on boundary cases. We therefore interpret this follow-up as evidence that a substantial part of the earlier disagreement reflected genuine clinician disagreement with benchmark or model recommendations rather than systematic misparsing by the automated judges.

**Source-stripped original wording**

To isolate the role of benchmark artifacts, we removed the original paper’s answer-formatting instructions while preserving the original wording and information order of the canonical cases. This source-stripped condition performed much closer to the exact structured benchmark than the fully naturalized free-text condition did. On matched rows, source-stripped originals achieved `79.2%` under GPT adjudication, `82.4%` under Claude adjudication, and `80.8%` on the two-judge mean, compared with `81.8%` for the exact structured benchmark. Thus, removing the benchmark scaffold from the original wording closed most of the gap that appeared when the same cases were rewritten into realistic patient language.

This result is important for interpretation. It suggests that much of the performance loss observed in the fully naturalized free-text condition is attributable to the shift into realistic patient phrasing itself, rather than simply to removal of the paper’s explicit response scaffolding. Put differently, the exact structured benchmark and the source-stripped original wording are behaviorally much closer to one another than either is to a fully naturalized patient-style message.

**Naturalistic forced-letter output**

We next tested a naturalistic condition that preserved patient-language input while constraining the model to return a categorical triage label on the paper’s scale. This was the strongest condition in the replication track. Against the exact structured benchmark, clinician-v2 natural forced-letter achieved `85.2%` versus `82.2%` on matched rows (`p = 0.0171`, Wilcoxon; McNemar exact `p = 0.00289`). Against pure natural free-text, natural forced-letter achieved `84.6%` versus `78.9%` on the two-judge mean (`p = 0.000170`, Wilcoxon).

This pattern implies that the most damaging component is not patient-language input alone. Rather, performance degrades most when naturalistic input is paired with unconstrained free-text output that must later be adjudicated back onto a categorical scale. When naturalistic input is retained but output is explicitly discretized, accuracy improves substantially. In the replication track, this makes the naturalistic forced-letter condition the strongest overall performer.

**Prompt-scaffold extension**

As a separate extension rather than a core replication condition, we evaluated a richer clinician-style prompt scaffold (`Prompt A v3`) on the canonical benchmark. This intervention was highly model-dependent. The strongest results were observed in the OpenAI models, with `gpt-5.3-instant` reaching `90.0%` and `gpt-5.4-xhigh` reaching `88.3%`. Claude non-thinking variants were also strong (`85.0%` each for Sonnet and Opus). By contrast, thinking-enabled Claude variants underperformed their non-thinking counterparts, and `claude-opus-4.6` with thinking deteriorated markedly to `48.3%`, driven by pervasive over-triage. The prompt-scaffolding extension therefore demonstrates that richer prompting can improve triage behavior in some models, but that its effect depends strongly on model family and reasoning mode.

**Relation to the original preprint**

These full canonical results support the core intuition of the original preprint, but they refine it substantially. In the original `17`-case/custom-bank study, matched natural interaction outperformed constrained evaluation (`70.1%` vs `63.6%`), DKA remained `100%` in both conditions, and asthma improved materially (`37/50` to `45/50`, with the realistic asthma prompt improving from `12/25` to `20/25`). On the full canonical clinician-v2 replication, however, the broad claim that natural free-text interaction rescues performance does not hold. Instead, exact structured outperformed fully naturalized free-text (`82.5%` vs `79.4%`), source-stripped originals nearly matched exact structured (`80.8%`), and naturalistic forced-letter outperformed both (`85.2%` vs `82.2%` exact structured; `84.6%` vs `78.9%` pure natural).

The strongest defensible conclusion is therefore narrower and more precise than the original preprint’s framing. The data do not support the claim that free-text naturalization uniformly improves triage. They do support the claim that benchmark behavior is highly sensitive to input and output format, that the original structured scaffold is not a neutral readout of underlying triage capability, and that a substantial portion of the apparent performance shift can be explained by the interaction between wording style, response discretization, and benchmark scaffolding.
