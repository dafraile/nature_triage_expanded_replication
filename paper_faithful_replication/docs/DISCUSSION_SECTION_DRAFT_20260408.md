**Discussion**

Our results argue against treating the original structured benchmark scaffold as a neutral measure of underlying triage capability. The strongest evidence comes from the dissociation between three closely related conditions. When the original paper wording was preserved but the benchmark’s explicit answer scaffold was removed, performance nearly matched the exact structured benchmark. When cases were rewritten into realistic patient language and left unconstrained in free-text, performance fell modestly. When naturalistic input was retained but output was discretized again onto the paper’s triage scale, performance improved and exceeded the exact structured benchmark. This pattern is difficult to reconcile with a simple “models are poor at triage” interpretation. It is much more consistent with a format-sensitive measurement problem in which input naturalization, output structure, and benchmark scaffolding each exert independent effects on apparent performance.

This updated full-canonical picture both supports and narrows the original preprint claim. The preprint, based on a smaller custom bank, suggested that natural free-text interaction could rescue apparent triage failures. The full canonical replication does not support that claim in general form: fully naturalized free-text remained modestly worse than exact structured. However, the broader mechanistic intuition survives. Benchmark behavior is highly sensitive to how cases are phrased and how responses are elicited and scored. In that sense, the new data do not overturn the original argument so much as sharpen it: the strongest general claim is not that natural free-text uniformly improves performance, but that the benchmark’s structured scaffold should not be interpreted as a transparent readout of clinical competence.

This point requires an additional qualification. Our “naturalistic” condition is not truly natural in the real-world sense. These prompts are still single-turn, self-contained, researcher-authored depictions of disease. They are more realistic than a structured clinical vignette, but they remain an exam-like proxy: the case is still pre-packaged, bounded, and stripped of the multi-turn clarification process through which real triage normally unfolds. In actual consumer use, what matters is not only how symptoms are initially phrased, but how the system elicits missing history, clarifies ambiguity, and adapts to new information over several turns. Our naturalistic condition therefore should be interpreted as a partial realism intervention, not as a faithful simulation of real-world chatbot triage.

The split between with-data and symptoms-only cases is also informative. In clinician-validated natural free-text, cases with objective data performed substantially better than symptoms-only cases, and under-triage was concentrated disproportionately in the symptoms-only subset. This suggests that the main challenge under realistic patient phrasing is not merely lexical variability, but uncertainty management when the presentation is sparse. In contrast, with-data cases tended to skew toward over-triage rather than under-triage, implying that once objective markers are present, models more often err on the side of escalation. This asymmetry is clinically important because it shows that the naturalistic free-text condition is not uniformly less safe; rather, it redistributes error in a way that depends on the informational structure of the case.

The naturalistic forced-letter condition is particularly revealing. Its superiority over both exact structured and pure natural free-text indicates that much of the performance loss in the fully naturalized setting arises downstream of input interpretation, at the stage where free-text recommendations must be inferred, adjudicated, and mapped back onto the paper’s discrete triage scale. In other words, some of the apparent deficit in naturalistic free-text may be a measurement artifact introduced by the need to recover a single categorical recommendation from richer prose. This is one reason we think benchmark studies should report not only category-level accuracy but also the degree of adjudicator disagreement and the distribution of under- versus over-triage errors.

The clinician adjudication follow-up partly sharpens this point. On the discrepant subset, the clinician's "literal model recommendation" aligned with at least one automated judge on every reviewed row and with both judges on most rows, suggesting that the automated adjudicators were generally not hallucinating categories that the responses did not support. The more persistent disagreement lay in the clinician's own preferred triage category, which often diverged from both the model-implied category and the benchmark gold. We therefore treat the follow-up as support for the plausibility of the automated adjudicators, but not as a formal blinded validation study. More broadly, it reinforces that some apparent "adjudication disagreement" in clinical benchmarking is actually disagreement about the underlying clinical standard rather than disagreement about what the model said.

In that sense, one of the central virtues of the original preprint does survive: the ablation logic. The current full-canonical program still decomposes the benchmark into behaviorally active components, but it now does so more cleanly. Instead of relying only on the earlier 17-case format comparison, the canonical study now includes a layered decomposition across (i) exact structured wording, (ii) source-stripped original wording, (iii) fully naturalized patient-style wording, (iv) naturalized input with forced categorical output, and (v) scaffolded prompting. This is still an ablation program, but a more mature one: it isolates not just “structured versus natural,” but the distinct contributions of benchmark wording, output discretization, and prompt scaffolding.

Our prompt-scaffold extension further underscores that prompt format is not a passive wrapper. A richer clinician-style scaffold materially improved some models, especially OpenAI systems, while degrading others, particularly thinking-enabled Claude variants. The collapse of Claude Opus thinking into pervasive over-triage suggests that “more reasoning” is not equivalent to better calibration. Instead, model family, reasoning mode, and prompt design interact in ways that can either attenuate or amplify safety-biased escalation. This is directly relevant to clinical benchmarking: a benchmark that is sensitive to such interactions should be interpreted as a test of the full model-prompt-evaluation system, not of the model alone.

These results also help contextualize the headline emergency claims in the earlier literature. The cases that drove the strongest original concerns, particularly DKA and asthma, do not behave identically under alternative conditions. DKA does not appear to be a stable general LLM failure mode, especially once explicit metabolic data are present. Asthma remains more fragile, particularly in symptoms-only formulations. This asymmetry matters. It suggests that aggregated “emergency under-triage” claims can conceal substantial heterogeneity across emergency constructs and across prompt conditions. Future evaluations should therefore avoid over-interpreting pooled emergency failure rates when those rates are dominated by a small number of scenario types or by behaviorally active prompt components.

This work has limitations. We still evaluate single-turn interactions rather than true multi-turn consumer use, and we rely on frontier APIs rather than the exact deployed product stack used in the original clinical study. Our naturalistic rewrites, although clinician-reviewed, remain artificial proxies for real patient interactions rather than complete simulations of them. In addition, adjudicated category labels remain an imperfect compression of richer natural-language advice. Nonetheless, the core empirical result is robust across the analyses presented here: triage performance is highly format-sensitive, and the original structured benchmark scaffold materially shapes the behavior it purports to measure. For that reason, conclusions about real-world consumer safety should not be drawn from a single structured evaluation format without systematic sensitivity analyses across input wording, output format, and opportunities for multi-turn clarification.
